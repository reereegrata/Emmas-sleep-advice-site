Baby Sleep Regressions, Ages, Signs & Survival Guide

By Emma · August 2026 · 11 min read

A sleep regression is a period when a baby who previously slept well suddenly starts waking more often at night, fighting naps, or becoming harder to settle. It affects nearly every baby at some point. According to the Raising Children Network, sleep regressions are linked to developmental leaps and typically occur at predictable ages: 4 months, 6 months, 8-10 months, 12 months, 18 months, and 2 years.

What Is a Sleep Regression?

A sleep regression is a temporary period, typically lasting two to six weeks, where a baby who had been sleeping well suddenly starts waking frequently at night, fighting naps, or becoming harder to settle at bedtime. Sleep regressions are not a sign that you have done something wrong as a parent. They are a normal part of development, triggered by the neurological changes happening inside your baby's brain as they learn new skills and process the world around them.

The term "regression" can be misleading. Your baby is not going backwards; their sleep is being temporarily disrupted because their brain is busy making major developmental leaps. Think of it as an upgrade in progress. The system goes offline for a bit while new features are being installed. Once the leap is complete, most babies return to their previous sleep patterns. Though after the 4-month regression, sleep patterns change permanently because of how sleep cycles mature.

Why Do Sleep Regressions Happen?

Sleep regressions are driven by brain development. At certain ages, your baby's brain undergoes significant rewiring as they acquire new skills: rolling, sitting, crawling, walking, talking. This rewiring is so intense that it disrupts the brain's ability to regulate sleep smoothly.

During a developmental leap, your baby's brain practises new skills around the clock, including during sleep. Separation anxiety also peaks at certain ages (8-10 months and 18 months), making it harder for your baby to settle without you. Physical factors like teething and growth spurts often overlap with regressions, which can make them feel more intense.

Red Nose safety reminder: During sleep regressions, it is more important than ever to maintain safe sleep practices. Baby on their back, in their own cot, with a firm mattress and no loose bedding or soft toys. The exhaustion of a regression can tempt parents to bring baby into bed. But this increases SIDS risk. See Red Nose safe sleep guidelines for the full checklist.

Not every baby hits every regression. Some sail through the 6-month regression but struggle at 18 months. Others have a tough 4-month regression but nothing after. Your baby's temperament, their sleep foundations before the regression hit, and how consistently you respond all play a part in how each phase unfolds.

Signs Your Baby Is Going Through a Sleep Regression

The hallmark is a sudden change in a baby who was previously sleeping well. Common signs:

  • Increased night waking. Going from waking once or not at all overnight to waking every one to two hours
  • Nap resistance. Naps they previously took without a fuss become a battle, or they skip them entirely
  • Shorter naps. That reliable 90-minute nap shrinks to 30 minutes, and your baby wakes up still tired and cranky
  • Increased fussiness and clinginess. Your baby wants to be held more during the day and becomes harder to put down at bedtime
  • Change in appetite. Some babies feed more during a regression due to an overlapping growth spurt; others are too distracted to feed well during the day and make up for it at night

The key word is sudden. If the change happened gradually, look for other causes: a slow shift in routine, accumulated overtiredness, or an underlying health issue. If your baby was sleeping well on Tuesday and everything fell apart on Wednesday, a regression is the most likely explanation.

Growth Spurt or Sleep Regression? How to Tell

Growth spurts and sleep regressions look similar, and they often overlap. Here is how to tell the difference:

  • Feeding behaviour. During a growth spurt your baby is genuinely hungry and feeds vigorously. During a regression they may wake and cry but settle quickly with comfort rather than a full feed
  • Duration. Growth spurts in younger babies last two to three days. Sleep regressions typically last two to six weeks
  • Daytime mood. Growth-spurt babies are often content between feeds. Regression babies tend to be fussier and clingier all day
  • Quick test. If a feed settles your baby within 10-15 minutes, you are probably dealing with a growth spurt. If feeding does not settle them, it is probably a regression

When both happen at once (common at 6 and 12 months), feed on demand during the day, keep bedtime consistent, and avoid introducing new sleep associations.

The 4-Month Sleep Regression

The 4-month sleep regression is the most significant one your baby will go through because it marks a permanent change in how their sleep cycles work. Before 4 months, babies have only two sleep stages: active (REM-like) and quiet (deep). Around 4 months, their sleep cycles mature into the four-stage pattern adults have: light sleep, deep sleep, and periods of partial waking between cycles.

This means your baby now wakes briefly between every sleep cycle, just like adults do, but unlike adults, they have not yet learned to roll over and go straight back to sleep. If they previously relied on being rocked or fed all the way to sleep, they now need that same help every time they surface between cycles, which can be every 45 to 90 minutes all night long.

For a step-by-step guide to managing this regression, see our dedicated 4-month sleep regression guide, which covers settling techniques, sample schedules, and when to consider sleep training for the first time.

6-Month Sleep Regression

The 6-month sleep regression catches many parents off guard because their baby had just settled after the 4-month regression. At six months, your baby is working through several milestones at once: rolling both ways, beginning to sit without support, possibly starting to crawl, and often starting solids for the first time. Each of these is a major neurological event, and your baby's brain practises them around the clock.

The most common 6-month pattern reported by Australian parents is frequent night waking where the baby has rolled onto their tummy and cannot roll back. Red Nose Australia advises that once a baby can roll independently, you do not need to reposition them onto their back during sleep, but you must continue placing them on their back at the start of every sleep. If your baby becomes distressed after rolling, go in, reassure them, and avoid turning this into a game. Give plenty of supervised tummy time during the day so they can practise rolling both ways.

Teething often coincides with this regression. If you suspect teething pain, baby paracetamol 20 minutes before bedtime (following the dosage for your baby's weight) can help break the pain-wake cycle. Always check with your GP or child health nurse first. Object permanence also develops around this age: your baby now understands you still exist when you leave the room, and they may protest loudly. A consistent bedtime routine and a calm goodbye at the cot help them learn you come back. See our 4-6 month sleep schedule for sample routines.

Most 6-month regressions pass within two to four weeks. If struggling after a month, check for underlying issues like reflux or ear infections. Karitane's free parent support line (1300 227 464) can help you decide whether a GP visit is needed.

Complete Sleep Regression Timeline: 8 Weeks to 2 Years

Not every baby hits every regression, but knowing what is coming can help you recognise the signs when they appear. Here is the full timeline:

8-Week Sleep Regression

At 8 weeks your baby is becoming much more alert. They may cluster feed in the evenings and fight daytime naps because they are too interested in what is going on. This phase usually lasts one to two weeks.

3-Month Sleep Regression

Some babies show signs of sleep disruption at 12 weeks as their sleep cycles begin the transition toward the 4-month shift. Treat it the same as the 4-month regression: consistent routine and drowsy-but-awake put-downs.

8-10 Month Sleep Regression

Mobility and separation anxiety drive this regression. Your baby is learning to crawl or pull to stand, and their brain is consumed with practising these skills around the clock. At the same time, separation anxiety peaks. Give your baby lots of floor time during the day and keep bedtime goodbyes short and confident.

11-12 Month Sleep Regression

Around their first birthday, many babies drop from two naps to one, and the transition can disrupt night sleep. If your baby fights the second nap but cannot manage a full day on one nap, try bringing bedtime forward to 6:00-6:30pm to prevent overtiredness. Our 10-12 month schedule guide has detailed nap transition advice.

14-15 Month Sleep Regression

By 14 months your toddler is walking confidently and beginning to test boundaries. Nap refusal is common. Hold firm on one nap per day at a consistent time: dropping the nap entirely almost always backfires with night waking from overtiredness.

18-Month Sleep Regression

Often the hardest after the 4-month one. Your toddler's language is exploding, separation anxiety returns, and they are developing a strong will. The best approach: a short, predictable bedtime routine, a firm "it's sleep time now" delivered with warmth, and zero negotiation. Giving in to "one more story" teaches your toddler that bedtime is flexible.

2-Year Sleep Regression

At two, sleep disruption often comes from big transitions: cot to bed, toilet training, or a new sibling. Imagination takes off, bringing fears of the dark or monsters. A night light, consistent routine, and calm response to night waking all help. Our toddler sleep guide covers cot-to-bed transitions in detail.

How Long Do Sleep Regressions Last?

Most sleep regressions last two to six weeks, with the average being three to four weeks. The 4-month regression tends to last the longest because it is a permanent neurological shift rather than a temporary phase. Regressions driven by a single new skill (like rolling at 6 months) often resolve faster than those driven by multiple factors (like the 18-month regression).

If a regression lasts longer than six weeks, look beyond developmental causes. Persistent sleep disruption can signal reflux, food intolerances, enlarged adenoids, or chronic ear infections. Tresillian's Parent Help Line (1300 272 736) offers free 24/7 phone support for Australian families.

The ROUTINE Method: Your Sleep Regression Survival Plan

When you are in the thick of a regression at 3am, you need a clear framework. The ROUTINE method gives you that:

  • R: Recognise the signs. Is this a regression, a growth spurt, or teething? Check the signs above. Knowing what you are dealing with stops you from trying fixes that will not work
  • O: Own the routine. Whatever else falls apart, protect the bedtime routine. Bath, book, feed, bed: same order, same time, same calm energy
  • U: Understand it is temporary. Remind yourself: "This is a phase. It will pass." Your baby is not broken, and neither are you
  • T: Tag in support. Call your partner, your mum, your best friend, or Tresillian (1300 272 736). A single four-hour stretch of sleep while someone else handles the baby can reset your entire outlook
  • I: Ignore the noise. Well-meaning relatives and online forums will tell you to try cry-it-out or co-sleep. During a regression, the only thing that matters is consistency with what was already working
  • N: No new habits. Do not introduce sleep associations you will have to undo later: feeding to sleep, driving around the block at 2am, or bringing baby into your bed. Comfort them, yes. Create a harder problem for next month, no
  • E: Evaluate after two weeks. If things are not improving after two weeks of consistent response, check for teething, illness, or schedule problems. Call your child health nurse for a second set of eyes

Can You Sleep Train During a Sleep Regression?

You can, but most Australian child health organisations advise waiting until the regression has passed. During a regression your baby genuinely needs more comfort than usual. Their brain is in overdrive, and they are not being difficult on purpose. Starting formal sleep training at this point is harder on both of you.

If you were already sleep training when the regression hit, pause any method that involves increased crying and switch to responsive settling: stay with your baby, offer comfort, and aim for drowsy-but-awake put-downs without pushing for full independence. You can resume where you left off once the phase passes. See our sleep training methods guide for a full comparison of approaches.

Common Mistakes Parents Make During Regressions

  1. Changing too many things at once. A regression is not the time to drop the dummy, move to a big bed, and start toilet training. One change at a time. During a regression, change nothing you do not have to
  2. Introducing a new sleep crutch. Feeding to sleep or rocking for 40 minutes every wake-up might get you through tonight, but you will still be doing it when the regression ends. Comfort without creating a habit you cannot sustain
  3. Dropping naps too early. A baby fighting naps during a regression still needs those naps. An overtired baby sleeps worse, not better
  4. Comparing your baby to others. Some babies breeze through regressions. Others do not. Your baby's experience says nothing about your parenting
  5. Skipping safe sleep rules because you are exhausted. No matter how tired you are, baby goes on their back, in their own cot, with a firm mattress. Red Nose Australia's guidelines do not take a holiday during regressions

When to See a GP or Child Health Nurse

Most sleep regressions pass on their own, but see your GP or child health nurse if:

  • The regression lasts longer than six weeks with no improvement
  • Your baby seems distressed or in pain, not just wakeful (inconsolable crying, back arching, pulling at ears)
  • Your baby's breathing sounds noisy or laboured during sleep
  • You are so exhausted that your own mental health is suffering. This matters, and help is available

Free Australian support lines:

  • Tresillian Parent Help Line: 1300 272 736 (24/7, free, staffed by child and family health nurses)
  • Karitane Care Line: 1300 227 464 (free parenting support, Australia-wide)
  • Pregnancy, Birth and Baby: 1800 882 436 (government-funded, 24/7 maternal child health advice)
  • Red Nose Australia: 1300 998 698 (safe sleep information and bereavement support)
